## Abstract

Stochastic resonance is said to be observed when increases in levels of unpredictable fluctuations — e.g., random noise — cause an increase in a metric of the quality of signal transmission or detection performance, rather than a decrease. This counterintuitive effect relies on system nonlinearities and on some parameter ranges being "suboptimal." The authors review the history, definitions, misconceptions, and debates surrounding SR, and argue that the brain likely exploits random noise as part of neural coding. They advocate reconceptualizing SR as "signal-processing noise benefits" and present six recommendations for biologists studying SR and neuronal variability.

## Key Findings
- **Formal definition:** SR occurs when noise INCREASES signal-to-noise ratio in a nonlinear system
- The brain likely exploits noise for functional advantage (not just tolerates it)
- SR requires: (1) a weak subthreshold signal, (2) noise, (3) a nonlinear threshold mechanism
- The inverted-U function: performance peaks at intermediate noise, drops at both extremes
- Diverse biological systems exploit randomness: sensory neurons, crayfish mechanoreceptors, human tactile/auditory/visual systems
- SR is not an artifact — it is a genuine, exploitable physical phenomenon with engineering applications

## Relevance to Aureonoise

This review provides the theoretical framework for why Aureonoise's noise is therapeutic, not just cosmetic. The brain operates near criticality (self-organized critical state) where noise plays a functional role in signal processing. Aureonoise's pink noise at -15 to -20 dB below the coherent signal (grain texture) targets the SR sweet spot. The review's emphasis on system nonlinearity maps to the brain's threshold-based neural firing: noise pushes subthreshold signals (weak but coherent "self" patterns) above firing threshold, while poorly integrated patterns (pathological "non-self") remain subthreshold or get swamped.
