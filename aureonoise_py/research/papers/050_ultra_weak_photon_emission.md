## Abstract
Cells emit light at ultra-low intensities: photons which are produced as by-products of cellular metabolism, distinct from other light emission processes such as delayed luminescence, bioluminescence, and chemiluminescence. The phenomenon is known by a large range of names, including biophotons, biological autoluminescence, metabolic photon emission and ultraweak photon emission (UPE). Research of UPE has a long yet tattered past, historically hamstrung by a lack of technology sensitive enough to detect it. Today, as technology progresses rapidly, it is becoming easier to detect and image these photons, as well as to describe their function.

## Key Findings
- UPE originates primarily from reactive oxygen species (ROS) reactions in mitochondria
- Detection intensity: tens to several hundred photons per second per cm-squared
- PMTs (photomultiplier tubes) provide optimal detection
- Applications span agriculture, food quality, dermatology, and neuroscience
- UPE may carry biological information beyond metabolic byproduct

## Relevance to Aureonoise
UPE (biophoton) research represents the frontier of biophysical mechanisms. While Aureonoise operates through auditory pathways, the biophoton literature connects to the broader question of how biological systems communicate through wave-based mechanisms. If neural biophotons carry information (paper 051), then the brain has native optical communication channels that could theoretically interact with or be modulated by external electromagnetic stimulation. This is speculative but relevant to Aureonoise's theoretical foundation.
